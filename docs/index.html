Fitting waves for every participant…

What participants did in each phase

Everyone ran the same 31-minute session in the MATB-II flight-task simulator, always in this order. Their main job was the fuel task: keep tanks A and B as close to 2,500 units as possible by switching pumps that move fuel from the reserve tanks.

  1. RestResting baseline · 1 min

    Sat still and relaxed with eyes open. No task on screen. This records the person's calm, untasked pupil.

  2. WorkWorking baseline · 6 min

    Did the fuel task alone, at a normal pace. Same screen and effort as the later phases, but without the stressors, so it is the fairest control.

  3. Stress 16 min

    Fuel task plus two stressors: four pumps failed every 10 s, and air-traffic-control radio messages arrived about three times a minute. Each call meant finding their own call sign and tuning a radio to the requested frequency.

  4. Recov 1Recovery 1 · 6 min

    Back to the fuel task alone. Shows how quickly the body settles after stress.

  5. Stress 26 min

    The same stress conditions again. Comparing it with Stress 1 shows whether people adapt or tire on a second exposure.

  6. Recov 2Recovery 2 · 6 min

    Fuel task alone once more, the final cool-down of the session.

Timings come from the dataset's protocol. Event markers in each recording show when each phase began.

Does stress change the pupil wave?

Each participant's recording was cut into overlapping 30-second windows. In each window the hippus signal was fitted with y = A·sin(B(t − C)) + D, and every constriction trough with y = a(t − h)² + k. Medians per phase are compared with a paired t-test. The headline number is Stress minus Working baseline, because both use the same screen and light. Each card also gives the effect size d and a 95% confidence interval.

Analyzing participants…

Why is the resting pupil the largest?

Stress should widen the pupil, yet the Resting baseline usually shows the biggest pupil of the session. The cause is light. The pupil reacts to brightness far more strongly than to thinking: light can change its size by 2–4 mm, while mental effort or stress adds only about 0.1–0.5 mm.

What changed between phases

  • Resting baseline: participants relaxed with eyes open and were not working on the bright task screen. Less light reached the eye, so the pupil was larger.
  • Working, Stress and Recovery: everyone stared at the same bright MATB-II screen, so the pupil shrank at once and stayed smaller for the rest of the session.
  • Fatigue and time order: resting always came first. Over 30 minutes the pupil slowly drifts smaller as people tire, which pulls every later phase down.

Comparing Rest with Stress therefore mostly compares two lighting conditions. The fair control is the Working baseline: the same screen, light and task, with only the stressors (radio calls and pump failures) added.

Mean pupil diameter by phase

For the hypothesis: the cards above therefore use Working as the main comparison. The "vs rest" line is shown for reference but is confounded by lighting. Lighting is listed as a control variable, but this dataset does not hold it constant across Rest and the task phases. This belongs in the project's limitations.

What happens the moment stress begins?

An event-locked average: every participant's pupil is lined up at the second the phase changed (time 0), measured as change from their own last 30 seconds before it, then averaged. The shaded band is the 95% confidence range of the average. If stress widens the pupil, the line should rise after 0.

Do more resilient people react less?

Each dot is one participant. Across: their CD-RISC resilience score. Up: how much the measure changed from Working to Stress. The line is the best-fit straight line, and r says how closely the dots follow it.

Research design

Can non-linear algebraic modeling, specifically sinusoidal wave equations and quadratic vertex analysis, mathematically tell apart pupil dynamics in healthy baseline subjects and subjects under acute cognitive stress, using open-source clinical data?

Independent variableCognitive state: baseline (resting or working) vs. acute stress.
Dependent variablesHippus wave amplitude A, wave constant B (frequency), and quadratic vertex curvature a during constriction.
Control variablesLighting, sampling rate (Hz) and recording duration, as fixed by the dataset.
HypothesisUnder acute stress, pupil oscillations show higher amplitude and higher frequency, seen as a significant rise in the wave constant B.

Where the numbers come from

Recordings are from the PhysioNet Neurophysiological Dataset of Stress Resilience During Human-Computer Interaction (v1.0.0): all 35 participants (p01–p37; p14 and p23 were excluded by the authors). Each participant's CSV holds 74 rows of signals sampled at 250 Hz. For p01–p24 this page uses rows 66–67 (right and left pupil diameter) and row 74 (event markers).

For p25–p37 the Tobii glasses recorded pupil size separately at 50 Hz (pXXeye.xlsx). Their event markers still come from row 74 of the main CSV. The two recordings share no clock, so they are lined up at the start of recording; phase boundaries for these 13 people may be off by up to about 30 s. When a session has extra markers, the last five are used, as the dataset notes advise. Resilience scores (CD-RISC, 0–40) come from demographicCDrisk.csv.

Participants ran the MATB-II resource-management task. After a 1-minute resting baseline, each condition lasted 6 minutes: Working baseline, Stress 1, Recovery 1, Stress 2, Recovery 2. Stress conditions added air-traffic radio requests and pump failures every 10 s.

Processing steps

  1. Clean. Average left and right pupil, drop values outside 1–10 mm (blinks, tracking loss), and average every 25 samples (250 Hz → 10 Hz). Gaps are bridged by straight lines, both for analysis and for the animated eye.
  2. Isolate the hippus. Smooth with a 0.5 s moving average, then subtract a 20 s moving average. What remains is the fast oscillation around the midline (D).
  3. Fit the sine. For every 30 s window, try each frequency from 0.10 to 1.00 Hz and solve the least-squares fit of a·sin(Bt) + b·cos(Bt). The best R² gives B; A = √(a² + b²) and C is the phase shift written as a horizontal shift.
  4. Fit the vertex. Find each trough that is the lowest point within ±0.8 s and at least 0.03 mm below the preceding peak. Fit y = at² + bt + c over ±0.6 s and convert it to vertex form; 2a is the turning acceleration in mm/s².
  5. Compare. Take the median of each metric per phase. The main test is a paired t-test of Stress (mean of Stress 1 and 2) against the Working baseline, reported with Cohen's d and a 95% confidence interval. Rest vs. Stress is reported too, but lighting differs between them.
  6. Time-lock and correlate. Average every participant's pupil change around each phase start, and correlate each person's Working-to-Stress change with their CD-RISC resilience score (Pearson r).

Controls and limits

Held constant by the dataset: same lab, same Tobii Pro Glasses 2 tracker, the same 250 Hz stream for p01–p24, and fixed 6-minute conditions. Screen brightness changes during the task can also move the pupil, so a size change is not purely cognitive.

Limits: p16's session stopped after 24 minutes, so its later phases are missing. The resting baseline is only 60 s long. The tracker's own rate is below 250 Hz, so the stream contains repeated values. The stress meter is an exploratory composite built for this page.

Glossary: every term in plain words

This project mixes eye science, algebra and statistics. Each term below is explained without assuming any background, with an everyday comparison where it helps. Search, or pick a topic.

The wave equation at a glance

y = A·sin(B(t − C)) + D

A is how tall each wave is, B sets how fast the waves repeat (T = 2π ÷ B), C slides the wave left or right, and D is the middle line the wave swings around.

The vertex of a constriction

y = a(t − h)² + k

When the pupil shrinks and then re-opens, the bottom of that dip looks like a U. The vertex (h, k) is the moment and size at the very bottom. A bigger a means a narrower, sharper U: the pupil turned around faster.
